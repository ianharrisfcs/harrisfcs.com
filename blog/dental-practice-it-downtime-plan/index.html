When dental software goes down: a practical IT downtime plan

A dental practice needs more than a backup. It needs a clear plan for continuing safe patient care when scheduling, imaging, phones, or the network stops working.

November 25, 20268 min readDental IT & Business Continuity
By Ian Harris, Founder of HarrisFCS · Last reviewed November 2026

The problem is not always the server

At 8:15 on a busy morning, the front desk cannot open the schedule. The operatories can still use their computers, but images will not load. Someone restarts a workstation, another person calls the software vendor, and nobody knows whether the problem is the network, the application, or a failed server.

That confusion can cost more time than the original fault. Dental offices depend on several connected systems, but those systems do not necessarily fail together. A practical downtime plan separates them, assigns responsibilities, and establishes what staff should do while the cause is investigated.

The objective is not to promise uninterrupted operation. It is to protect patient information, preserve clinical judgment, avoid making a technical failure worse, and restore the most important functions in a sensible order.

Identify the systems that keep appointments moving

Begin with a short inventory of operational dependencies. Include the practice management system, its database and server or cloud service, clinical imaging and acquisition software, sensor and scanner integrations, internet service, network equipment, phones, payment processing, printers, and secure communication channels.

For each system, record its owner, support contact, location, dependencies, and acceptable downtime. A locally hosted practice management database may depend on a server, storage, network, and working name resolution. A cloud application may be healthy while the office internet connection is down. A sensor may capture images correctly but fail to transfer them into the patient chart.

These distinctions matter. Restarting every computer will not fix an internet outage, and rebooting a database server without coordination can interrupt active work or complicate recovery.

Decide which functions come first

Restoration priorities should follow patient care and business impact, not whichever user reports the loudest problem. Clinical staff must decide whether a procedure can continue safely without a particular system; IT staff should not make clinical decisions.

For many practices, the immediate priorities are access to the day's schedule and essential patient information, safe communication with patients, clinical imaging needed for care, and then billing and other administrative work. The exact order depends on the services performed and whether an alternative workflow is available.

Agree in advance who can postpone appointments, authorize a temporary workflow, contact patients, and decide when normal operations resume. A downtime plan that names no decision-maker is only a reference document.

Prepare a secure paper or offline workflow

A practice should know what staff can do if the primary software is unavailable. This might include a carefully controlled appointment list prepared under an approved procedure, paper intake forms, a method for recording clinical information, and a documented process for entering it into the official record later.

Offline copies of patient information are sensitive records, not ordinary convenience files. Limit access, protect them from loss or casual viewing, track what was created, and dispose of temporary copies securely after reconciliation under the practice's retention and privacy procedures.

Do not improvise by sending records to personal email accounts or storing patient data in unapproved consumer applications. A workaround that restores convenience while exposing information creates a second incident.

Separate application, network, and device failures

When something stops working, start with scope. Is the problem affecting one workstation, one operatory, all users, or only remote locations? Does the network still work? Can staff reach other applications? Are the server and storage healthy? Did the failure begin after an update, power event, or configuration change?

Record the first observed symptoms, exact error messages, affected devices, and changes already attempted. This gives the internal technician or outside provider a useful starting point and reduces duplicate troubleshooting.

A single imaging workstation failing to acquire an image is different from the imaging database being unavailable across the office. Likewise, a payment terminal outage should not automatically trigger changes to the practice management server. Troubleshoot the smallest affected component first unless evidence indicates a broader failure.

Understand imaging as a separate recovery problem

Dental imaging can involve acquisition devices, drivers, calibration files, imaging databases, local caches, and integration with the practice management system. A working patient chart does not prove that every image is protected or accessible.

Document where original images reside, how they are linked to patients, whether acquisition stations store temporary data, and what configuration or calibration material would be required to rebuild a workstation. Verify with the relevant application provider how to back up and restore its database and image repository consistently.

After a restore, test more than the ability to open the application. Confirm that the appropriate patient records, historical images, and integration functions are available. Do not test using live clinical acquisition in a way that could affect patient care.

Backups need a recovery sequence

Backups are essential, but they do not explain how a practice will operate while recovery takes place. A useful recovery plan identifies what must be restored first, where the backup is stored, which credentials are required, and whether replacement hardware or a clean environment will be available.

Use protected backup copies that are not all exposed to the same failure or credentials. Monitor jobs, investigate failures, and periodically restore representative files and applications to a safe test environment. For database-backed software, coordinate application-consistent backups and restoration procedures with the vendor.

Ask two business questions: how much recent work could be lost, and how long could the office tolerate the service being unavailable? Those answers inform the recovery point objective and recovery time objective. Neither should be invented by an IT provider without the practice's input.

Plan for internet, phone, and power failures

Not every outage calls for restoring a server. If the internet connection fails, cloud scheduling, electronic claims, payment processing, and hosted phones may all be affected. Identify whether a secondary connection or cellular backup is appropriate and which systems can use it.

Document how phones will be forwarded, how staff will contact scheduled patients, and how the practice will communicate a delay without revealing private information. A backup connection is only useful if the router, firewall, switching equipment, and power arrangements support the intended failover.

For power interruptions, understand which equipment is protected by battery backup and for how long. A UPS can support orderly shutdown or brief continuity; it is not a substitute for a tested power strategy.

Know what HIPAA actually requires

For covered entities and business associates, the HIPAA Security Rule requires contingency planning for emergencies or other events that damage systems containing electronic protected health information. Its requirements include data backup, disaster recovery, and emergency-mode operation plans. Testing and revision procedures and applications-and-data criticality analysis are addressable specifications, which require an appropriate documented determination rather than being casually ignored.

The rule is technology-neutral. It does not prescribe one backup product, one server configuration, or one universal recovery time for every dental practice. HHS guidance emphasizes reasonable and appropriate safeguards based on the organization's circumstances and risks.

For additional detail, see the HHS Security Rule summary and HHS ransomware and contingency guidance.

Give the front desk a one-page response guide

Technical recovery documentation can be detailed, but the first page used during an outage should be short. Staff need to know whom to call, what symptoms to record, what not to restart, which temporary workflows are approved, and who decides whether to reschedule care.

  • Identify the affected systems and the time the problem began.
  • Notify the designated office lead and IT contact.
  • Preserve error messages and avoid repeated uncoordinated restarts.
  • Use only approved patient-information and communication procedures.
  • Escalate clinical decisions to the appropriate licensed staff.
  • Record work performed during downtime for later reconciliation.
  • Confirm restoration and data accuracy before closing the incident.

Keep essential contact details somewhere accessible even if the office's main systems are offline. Review the guide after staffing, software, or infrastructure changes.

Practice the plan before an actual outage

Start with a tabletop exercise: imagine the practice management system is unavailable at opening time. Ask the office manager, clinical lead, and IT provider what they would do in the first fifteen minutes, first hour, and remainder of the day.

Then test technical recovery separately in a controlled environment. Record how long restoration takes, what information is missing, which vendor dependencies slow the process, and which instructions were unclear. Update the plan and assign follow-up work.

Most practices do not need an elaborate disaster simulation. They need evidence that the people, systems, and procedures can work together when the normal workflow fails.

Make downtime planning part of routine IT management

Continuity planning works best when it is maintained alongside device inventories, patching, backups, account management, and vendor documentation. New imaging equipment, a software migration, a network change, or a new location can alter the recovery plan substantially.

For dental practices, the useful outcome is straightforward: staff know how to respond, patient information remains protected, clinical leadership retains control of care decisions, and technical recovery follows an understood sequence.
