Cybersecurity & Vendor Risk

Vendor access is a security decision: a small-business checklist.

Outside vendors often need legitimate access to systems or sensitive information. The risk comes when that access is broader, longer-lived, or less visible than anyone intended.

October 21, 20268 min readCybersecurity & Vendor Risk
By Ian Harris, Founder of HarrisFCS · Last reviewed October 2026

Small businesses depend on outside vendors. Payroll providers, accounting platforms, software support teams, IT providers, payment processors, cloud services, copier companies, industry-specific software vendors, and consultants may all touch business systems or data.

That is normal. The problem is not that vendors have access. The problem is when nobody can clearly answer four questions: Who has access? To what? Why? And when does that access end?

Vendor risk management does not need to become a procurement bureaucracy. For most small organizations, a short, repeatable process can eliminate a surprising amount of avoidable exposure.

Start by identifying vendors that actually matter

Not every supplier deserves the same security review. The company that delivers office furniture is different from the company that can remotely administer your server.

Prioritize vendors that can access sensitive information, administrative accounts, endpoints, networks, backups, email, financial systems, regulated data, or other critical business services.

NIST Cybersecurity Framework 2.0 specifically addresses supply-chain risk. Its guidance includes performing due diligence before entering important supplier relationships, understanding and monitoring supplier risk during the relationship, including relevant suppliers in incident planning, and planning for what happens when the relationship ends.

For a small business, that can start with a simple vendor inventory: vendor name, service provided, business owner, systems or data involved, access level, renewal date, and whether the vendor still needs access.

Ask what the vendor actually needs

Convenience tends to expand permissions. A vendor asks for an administrator account because it makes support easier. A temporary remote-access tool remains installed for years. A shared login becomes the permanent way to access a portal.

Instead, define the minimum access needed to perform the work.

If a vendor only supports one application, it may not need unrestricted administrative access across every workstation. If access is needed once for a project, it may not need to remain enabled indefinitely. If the vendor has multiple technicians, a shared generic administrator account makes accountability harder than named or otherwise attributable access.

The principle is simple: access should match the job.

Protect vendor accounts like privileged accounts

A vendor account with administrative access is a privileged account, regardless of whose employee uses it.

That means strong authentication matters. Use MFA where supported, avoid password reuse, limit administrative privileges, and do not casually share internal administrator credentials with outside companies.

Where practical, maintain separate vendor access rather than allowing an outside technician to use the same account your internal administrator uses. Separation improves accountability and makes it easier to remove access without disrupting someone else.

For especially sensitive access, consider whether it can be enabled only when needed rather than remaining permanently available.

Remote access deserves particular attention

Remote support is valuable because a technician can solve problems without traveling to the office. The same capability can also provide a path into business systems.

Know which remote-access tools are installed, who controls them, how users authenticate, and whether old tools remain from previous vendors.

When changing IT providers or ending a software support relationship, removing remote-access agents should be part of the offboarding process. Do not assume uninstalling one visible application removes every management agent, service account, VPN profile, API integration, or administrative credential associated with the relationship.

This is one reason good managed IT includes asset visibility and documentation rather than relying on institutional memory.

Understand what information the vendor receives

Some vendors never log into your network but still receive sensitive information. Cloud applications, payroll systems, document portals, backup providers, and outsourced business processes can all store business or customer data.

Ask what information is collected, where it goes, who can access it, how long it is retained, and what happens to it when the service ends. The depth of review should reflect the sensitivity of the data and importance of the service.

NIST's current supply-chain guidance emphasizes due diligence and risk assessment rather than treating every supplier identically. That is a useful model for small businesses: spend more attention on relationships that create more meaningful exposure.

Put important expectations in the agreement

A sales conversation is not a security control.

For important providers, contracts or service agreements should address the security expectations appropriate to the relationship. Depending on the service, that may include protection of sensitive information, access controls, incident notification, subcontractors, data return or deletion, availability expectations, and responsibilities when the agreement ends.

NIST CSF 2.0 explicitly calls for cybersecurity supply-chain requirements to be integrated into contracts and other agreements with relevant suppliers and third parties.

This does not mean every small business needs to write its own enterprise vendor contract. It does mean important security assumptions should not exist only as verbal promises.

For covered financial institutions, vendor oversight is also a compliance issue

The FTC Safeguards Rule applies to financial institutions under the FTC's jurisdiction, a category that can include tax preparation firms and other businesses engaged in covered financial activities. The Rule requires covered organizations to maintain safeguards for customer information and to take steps regarding service providers that handle that information.

FTC guidance says covered businesses must take reasonable steps to select and retain service providers capable of maintaining appropriate safeguards and require those safeguards by contract. The Rule also requires periodic assessment of service providers based on the risk they present and the continued adequacy of their safeguards.

For a tax or accounting firm, this is why vendor management belongs inside the broader WISP and security program. A list of software subscriptions is not the same as documented service-provider oversight.

Specific regulatory obligations depend on the organization and relationship, so compliance questions should be reviewed in context rather than assumed from a generic checklist.

Do not forget integrations and API access

Modern vendor access is not always a human logging into a computer.

An application may have permission to read email, access cloud files, synchronize contacts, manage users, connect to accounting data, or operate through an API token. These integrations can remain active long after employees forget why they were approved.

Periodically review connected applications and integrations in important cloud platforms. Remove those that are no longer used, verify the owner of those that remain, and pay particular attention to applications with broad permissions.

This should be part of normal Microsoft 365 or Google Workspace administration, not something discovered only during an incident.

Plan the exit before you need it

Vendor offboarding is where weak access management becomes obvious.

When a relationship ends, someone should know what needs to be removed or transferred. That may include user accounts, administrative roles, remote-management agents, VPN access, API keys, application integrations, shared passwords, physical keys, documentation, domain or cloud ownership, and stored business data.

NIST CSF 2.0 specifically includes supply-chain risk activities after a partnership or service agreement concludes. That is practical advice even when no regulation requires it.

A clean exit also reduces business continuity risk. Critical systems, domains, cloud tenants, and documentation should remain under the organization's control rather than becoming inaccessible because a former provider was the only administrator.

A practical vendor-access checklist

For each vendor with meaningful system or data access, ask:

  • What business service does this vendor provide?
  • What systems, accounts, or information can it access?
  • Is that access necessary for the work?
  • Is the access attributable rather than unnecessarily shared?
  • Is MFA enabled where available?
  • Does the vendor have more privilege than it needs?
  • What remote-access software or integrations are involved?
  • What sensitive information does the vendor store or process?
  • Are important security expectations documented in the agreement?
  • Who inside the business owns the relationship?
  • How would the vendor participate if an incident affected its service?
  • What needs to be revoked, returned, transferred, or deleted when the relationship ends?
  • When was the relationship last reviewed?

You do not need perfect answers before using any outside service. You do need enough visibility to make an informed decision.

Review vendors when something changes

A vendor that was low risk three years ago may now host more data, have deeper access, or support a more critical business process.

Review the relationship when scope changes, access expands, a contract renews, an incident occurs, or the vendor materially changes how the service works. Higher-risk vendors deserve more frequent attention than suppliers with no meaningful system or data access.

The goal is not paperwork. The goal is preventing forgotten access and unexamined dependencies from becoming permanent.

Vendor management is access management

For a small business, vendor risk becomes much easier to understand when it is treated as an extension of ordinary access management.

Know who can reach important systems and data. Give them only what they need. Protect the access. Document the relationship. Review it periodically. Remove it when the work ends.

That process is useful whether you are building a formal compliance program or simply trying to run a healthier technology environment.
