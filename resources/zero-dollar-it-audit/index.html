You don't need to be an IT expert to ask good questions.

This guide helps identify missing safeguards, unclear responsibilities, and obvious operational risks.

How to use the guide

  1. Set aside 45–90 minutes with whoever handles computers, accounts, vendors, or office operations.
  2. Answer all 25 checks. Mark Yes only when you can verify it. Otherwise mark No or Not sure.
  3. Gather evidence such as settings screenshots, inventories, procedures, or a successful restore test.
  4. Fix urgent exposures first, then plan remaining improvements over 30 days.
  5. Repeat the review quarterly and after major staff, software, or office changes.
Important boundary: This is a screening tool, not a penetration test, formal security assessment, legal opinion, or certification of compliance. Never share passwords, recovery codes, sensitive client records, or full configuration exports while completing it.

01 / 05

Know what you own

Unknown devices, accounts, and subscriptions are difficult to secure, support, or budget for.

01
Device inventory

Can you list every company computer, server, tablet, network device, and its assigned owner?

02
Software inventory

Can you identify the critical applications, subscriptions, renewal dates, and who administers them?

03
Supported systems

Are operating systems, browsers, and critical business applications still supported and receiving security updates?

04
Data map

Do you know where business-critical files and customer information are stored, including cloud services and employee devices?

05
Asset ownership

Can you identify who owns the domain name, website, email tenant, business phone numbers, and administrative accounts?

What to verify

Evidence to look for: device list with model, serial number, owner, location, warranty and last check date; subscription list with provider, purpose, billing owner and renewal; data locations including shared drives, cloud storage, laptops and removable media.

Common red flag: a former employee or outside vendor is the only person who knows how to access a critical business account.
02 / 05

Control access

Account security is often the cheapest place to reduce avoidable exposure.

06
Multifactor authentication

Is MFA enabled for business email, financial services, remote access, and administrative accounts?

07
Unique accounts

Does each person have their own login rather than sharing one account across staff?

08
Least privilege

Do employees have only the access required for their roles, with administrator rights restricted?

09
Staff departures

Is there a documented same-day process to disable accounts, revoke sessions, and recover devices when someone leaves?

10
Recovery readiness

Are recovery contacts, backup authentication methods, and emergency admin access documented and controlled?

What to verify

Safe verification: review sign-in dashboards for MFA exceptions without collecting secret codes; inspect access lists for departed staff and unexplained administrators; walk through a staff exit scenario.

Priority warning: missing MFA on business email or administrator accounts deserves prompt attention. Password strength alone is not an adequate substitute.
03 / 05

Keep computers healthy

Reliable endpoint management reduces both security risk and everyday disruption.

11
Automatic updates

Are operating-system and major application updates deployed promptly and monitored for failures?

12
Endpoint protection

Does every supported business computer have active, centrally monitored security protection?

13
Disk encryption

Are laptops and other portable devices encrypted, with recovery keys securely retained?

14
Screen and device security

Do computers lock automatically and require authentication after inactivity?

15
Local admin restrictions

Can ordinary users work without permanent local administrator privileges?

What to verify

Evidence to look for: update status and failed installations, protection dashboard coverage compared with the device inventory, and a controlled process for retrieving encryption recovery keys.

Common red flag: a computer appears fine but has not installed updates for months, or it is absent from the security monitoring dashboard.
04 / 05

Protect email and backups

A backup is not proven until data has been restored successfully.

16
Email authentication

Are SPF, DKIM, and DMARC configured and reviewed for the domains you send mail from?

17
Phishing reporting

Do employees know how to report suspicious messages and verify unusual payment or account-change requests?

18
Backup coverage

Are critical systems and cloud data included in a documented backup plan?

19
Backup isolation

Is at least one recovery copy protected against routine account compromise, deletion, or ransomware?

20
Restore testing

Has the business successfully restored representative files or systems within a defined, recent testing interval?

What to verify

Ask your backup provider: what systems and cloud data are covered, how far back you can recover, and how long restoration would take for the most important business process.

Priority warning: no recoverable backup of critical data deserves immediate attention. Use a controlled sample restore, not deletion of production data.
05 / 05

Network, vendors, and people

Good security depends on clear responsibility as much as technical controls.

21
Network separation

Are guest Wi-Fi and untrusted devices separated from business systems where appropriate?

22
Network equipment

Are router, firewall, and Wi-Fi firmware maintained, and are default administrator credentials changed?

23
Vendor access

Can you identify outside parties with remote or privileged access, and disable access when no longer needed?

24
Incident response

Does the team know whom to contact and what to do after a suspected compromise or major outage?

25
Ownership and review

Is one person accountable for technology risk, renewals, policy review, and follow-up on open issues?

What to verify

Quick tabletop exercise: imagine a ransom note appears and customer records become inaccessible at 9:15 a.m. Decide who responds in the first 15 minutes, who contacts IT and other advisers in the first hour, and which service must be restored first.

Common red flag: a vendor has permanent unattended access but nobody knows which accounts or devices it can reach.
Audit scorecard

What your score means

Mark Yes, No, or Not sure for each of the 25 checks. Only verified Yes responses earn two points, for a maximum of 50. This score is a planning aid, not a measure of breach probability.

40–50Many basic controls verified. Close remaining gaps and independently validate high-risk systems.
26–38Meaningful gaps or unknowns. Prioritize email, administrator access, backups, and unsupported systems.
0–24Foundational controls need attention. Create a short remediation plan and seek qualified help where needed.

Override the score when you find these

Urgent: missing recoverable backups, exposed privileged access, or an active compromise. High: unsupported critical systems.

A checklist does not establish compliance with HIPAA, the FTC Safeguards Rule, PCI DSS, or any other framework. Regulated businesses may require additional safeguards, documentation, and professional review.

Your 30-day improvement plan

Turn findings into action.

Assign each improvement an owner, evidence of completion, and a realistic deadline.

Days 1–3Resolve immediate exposure: compromised accounts, missing MFA on critical access, unusable backups, or active threats.
Week 1Document systems, key accounts, critical data, and outside vendors.
Week 2Correct identity and device-management gaps; review former staff and administrator permissions.
Week 3Verify backups with a controlled restore; confirm emergency contacts and recovery priorities.
Week 4Run a short incident exercise and assign quarterly review responsibilities.

Write down your three most important fixes, the accountable owner, and your next review date. The PDF provides dedicated space for this.

Questions to ask your IT provider

Ask for evidence, not promises.

CoverageHow do you verify that every business device is managed, patched, and protected?
IdentityWhich administrator accounts exist, who owns them, and how is MFA enforced?
BackupsWhat systems are included, and when was the last successful restore test?
IncidentsWhat happens in the first hour of a ransomware event or serious outage?
DependenciesWhich services and credentials are controlled by third parties, and how would they be transferred?
ReportingWhat risks remain unresolved, and how is progress reported?
OffboardingIf we change providers, how do we receive our accounts, documentation, licenses, and data?

A good answer identifies a responsible person, a process, when it was last verified, and evidence available to the business.

A little preparation goes a long way

Keep your completed scorecard, inventories, service owners, action plan, and restore-test evidence internally. Share high-level findings selectively, never passwords, recovery codes, or sensitive customer data. Revisit after significant changes or incidents.
